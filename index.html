How it works

Clicks become flashes. Each echolocation click lights a narrow cone ahead of your head (about 16° wide at −3 dB). Only what the beam hits is lit. Off-axis targets fade fast.

Range is short. Echoes lose strength on the way out and on the way back (40·log10R), and high-frequency sound is absorbed quickly. Small fish show up at tens of metres at most, rocks and the sea surface further away.

Limited output. Your average acoustic power is fixed. Clicking faster makes every click weaker, and loud clicks drain the OUTPUT bar. Narrowing the beam puts more level on-axis for the same effort.

Slow clicks reach further. Below 17 clicks a second, each click is louder and your beam narrower, so things show from further off: at 5 a second or slower, about 5 dB louder and 13° wide instead of 16°, together about 7 dB more on the beam's axis. A fish then shows from about 25 m instead of 17 m. Real toothed whales click louder and further apart when they're looking further off, lowering the level as they close in (Atem et al. 2009; Linnenschmidt et al. 2012); wild porpoises click at 178 to 205 dB, usually about 60 ms apart but up to 200 ms (Villadsgaard et al. 2007). Porpoise beams narrow by only a few degrees (Koblitz et al. 2012 measured 13° by 11°), so the game narrows yours a little. Faster than about 18 a second, your output can't keep up and each click weakens.

The buzz. As you close on prey, slide CLICKS up into the BUZZ zone (headset: hold the viewer's button). Let go and it drops back to your search click rate. Clicks speed up towards 500 per second, each one gets weaker and the beam widens, as real porpoises broaden their beam during the buzz (Wisniewska et al. 2015). Your view zooms out to match, so a fish that darts sideways stays in sight, and turning gets more sensitive so you can follow it. You get a fast-updating, short-range view for the final strike. Simulated time slows (to 0.25× by default) while your head still turns in real time. A buzz overspends your output budget. Set the beam widening, zoom, turn sensitivity and slow-down under Settings → Buzz.

Buzzes are short. You can't buzz all the time. The amber BUZZ line under OUTPUT holds 3 s of full buzz, counted in the porpoise's own time, and refills over 12 s, so over a longer stretch you can buzz at most a fifth of the time. When it runs dry the buzz stops: let go, and you can buzz again once it has refilled a quarter of the way. Real porpoise buzzes last about 1 to 3 s: 1.4 s on average for porpoises catching fish (DeRuiter et al. 2009) and 2.3 s when choosing between targets (Hermannsen et al. 2025). Wild porpoises make up to 550 buzzes an hour at night (Wisniewska et al. 2016), roughly a fifth of their time at 1.4 s each. Buzz clicks come 10 to 20 times as fast as search clicks but are about 10 to 30 dB weaker, so a buzz puts out at most about twice the sound energy per second of searching, and often far less. That's why the limit is on time spent buzzing rather than on sound energy. Change both numbers under Settings → Buzz.

Closing in. When a fish lit up in your beam is within about 6 m it gets an outline, brighter the nearer it is, and four corner brackets close in on it. When the brackets meet the outline the fish is within striking distance (2 m) and the outline turns amber: you lunge forward and draw the fish into your mouth. It's a game aid for judging distance, which is hard in the dark, above all from behind the porpoise or in a headset with no speed control. Turn it off, or change the striking distance, under Settings → Catching.

Click rate. Set it with the lower part of the CLICKS slider, from 50 per second down to 1 per second, or off to swim silently and just listen.

Lessons. The first levels are lessons, in a quiet spot with no hazards and, until level 6, no need to breathe. Level 1, Locomotion: weave between six posts along a rock channel, following the green arrows, and touch the ball at the end, first by daylight in clear water, then, after night falls, by biosonar with your clicks fixed at 3 a second. Level 2, Click rate: swim through five rings with your clicks fixed at 1 a second, a single glimpse then dark, then again setting your own click rate, up to 15 a second (in the headset, press the viewer button to click faster). Level 3, Beam and buzz: in shallow water, held in one spot just above the sandy floor, find nine balls around you. The far ones show best with slow clicks, louder and narrower; the close ones, one of them hanging from a buoy and swinging, are quickest to catch in the wide, short beam of a buzz. Hold a ball in or near your beam ring for a moment and it's ticked off in green (stay on a far one for longer, with it nearer the ring); then try a timed run. Level 4, What echoes well: swim to find seven objects of different materials. How strongly something echoes depends on how different it is from seawater in density and sound speed, and on its size: an air float, steel and rock show from far off, plastic and a jellyfish only close in, and a gillnet's thin twine closer still. The Pearl Net carries 8 mm acrylic beads, which resonate at porpoise click frequencies and, spaced less than half a metre apart, echo as strongly as the net's float line (Kratzer et al. 2020). Level 5, Chase: catch four fish one after another in a small rock pool, a big, slow cod first, then a whiting, a sprat and a herring, each faster and warier. Each bolts if you come at it fast or buzz near it, then pauses to recover, and tires a little with every bolt. Level 6, Breathing: from the surface of the pool, catch sand gobies near the bottom on one breath, then come up to breathe; each dive needs more points than the last. Tagged harbour porpoises' dives last 44 s to 1¾ minutes on average (Westgate et al. 1995), and they take three to seven breaths at the surface after each, more after longer dives (Watson & Gaskin 1983). Pick any level to start at on the title screen.

Prey and points. Seven kinds of prey live in different places and escape in different ways. Each catch scores points in proportion to the energy in one fish, about 25 kJ a point. Oily herring, sprat and sandeels carry more energy per gram than lean whiting and cod (Pedersen & Hislop 2001), and a big fish carries far more than a small one. Fish with a gas-filled swimbladder echo much more strongly than sandeels and squid, which have none, so they show up from further away.

Levels. After the lessons, reach each level's points target to move on, and come up to breathe. People's use of the sea builds up as you go: a gillnet first, then a boat with an echo sounder, then a trawler and passing shipping, then fewer fish. Hazards you switch off in Settings stay off, and you can turn levels off to swim freely.

Sounds are colours. Everything you can hear glows in a colour set by its frequency, from red for low rumbles to violet for the highest pitches, just as red light has a lower frequency than violet light. Sound reaches you from every direction, so a source off to the side or behind you glows at the edge of your view. Look straight at a glow to see what it is.

Hearing range. How bright a glow is, and how far away it still shows, follows a simple model: spreading and absorption on the way to you, compared with harbour porpoise hearing (Kastelein et al. 2002). Engine noise is low-pitched, so it carries for kilometres, but porpoises hear low frequencies poorly. Pingers and echo sounders are heard well but fade within a kilometre or two, because high frequencies are absorbed faster.

Air. When your air drops below half, your view starts to redden at the edges and you hear your heartbeat slow. Oxygen use rises with the cube of swim speed and is cheapest per metre at about 1.4 m/s, as measured in captive harbour porpoises (Otani et al. 2001). Clicking adds a little on top, in proportion to the sound energy you put out: about 10% at the default rate. That's based on bottlenose dolphins, whose clicking raised metabolic rate only about 1.1× over silence (Noren et al. 2017).

Seabed. Rippled sand gives a grainy echo, gravel and shell a speckled one, and soft mud in the gully to the east hardly echoes at all, apart from the burrows in it. A sandbank rises to the west, with sand waves on its flanks, and boulders are strewn over five rocky reefs. Beyond that middle 200 m, an outer ring takes the area to 400 m across, with eight more reefs and two more sandbanks. It gets shallower to the north, where the tops of the reefs and banks come up to about 10 m. Settings → World → Play area goes back to the original 200 m.

At the surface. When you come up to breathe your head clears the water, and you see the sky, the waves, any boats nearby and seabirds. Gulls and gannets circle over schools of herring and sprat, so birds tell you where to dive. You ride up and down with the waves. Each breath takes about 1.2 s, about as long as breathing out and in takes a porpoise (Amundin 1974). In calm water one breath fills your lungs; in rougher water each gives less, and a breaker washing over you spoils the breath you're taking, so you need more breaths before you dive again: in a storm, by default, a breath gives a third, and half your breaths are spoiled if you don't time them. From below you can see the waves within about 10–15 m of the surface, the sky through the window overhead tilting with the crests, and the white water of breakers, which also echoes strongly, and you can hear a breaker's roar as it comes: come up just behind one. Or come up fast and steeply to breach, leaving the water for a moment with a full breath. Set the sea state in Settings.

Gillnets. Thin monofilament mesh returns very weak echoes, strongest when you face it square-on. The float line and lead line show up before the mesh does. Touch the net and you're caught. There's a 50% chance (set in Settings) that you can break free, but only by turning round and swimming back the way you came. Otherwise you're held until your air runs out.

Trawls. A trawler tows a funnel-shaped net along the seabed at about 2 m/s, held open by two steel doors. You'll hear it long before you see it. If you end up inside the funnel, swim out of the mouth faster than the net is moving. The mesh walls can catch you, and the cod-end at the back traps you for good.

Echo sounders. The boat's 50 kHz sounder lights the scene with its own pings (green) and masks your echoes while you're in or near its beam.

Debrief. After a dive, or from the pause menu, the debrief maps where you swam and where your beam pointed, plots your dive profile with the seabed beneath you, and for each pass by the gillnet says how far away its floats, lead line and mesh first showed in your beam. Press ▶ or drag along the profile to replay the dive. It uses the same echo model as the game.

Follow behind. Settings → Display → View (also in the pause menu) can put you a few metres behind the porpoise instead of looking through its eyes, so you or people watching can see its beam sweep ahead of it. The porpoise is drawn dim, with a glowing edge: its own clicks point away from it, so they never light it. It's drawn smaller than life, and smaller still when the view is close, so its tail doesn't loom over the view; Settings → Display sets its size and how solid it looks.

Pingers (optional) sound on the gillnet. In interactive mode they only sound once they detect your clicks, and they can trigger their neighbours. Settings → World → Pingers sets how loud they are: a louder pinger is heard, and glows, from further away.

Controls. Headset: look to aim and steer, hold the viewer's button to buzz. Phone: drag to look, SWIM sets speed, CLICKS sets click rate (bottom = off) with BUZZ at the top. Keyboard: drag or arrows to look, W/S speed, A/D sideways, R/F up/down, hold Space to buzz, Q/E beam, [ ] click rate, −/= power, V to switch between seeing through the eyes and following behind, Esc menu. Gamepad: left stick speed and sideways, right stick look, RT (analog) or hold A to buzz, LB/RB beam, D-pad rate/power. Mouse (for example a Bluetooth mouse paired with the phone): main wheel click rate, thumb wheel or sideways scroll swim speed; in the headset, hold the left button to buzz, the right or forward button recentres and the back button opens the menu. To turn round without twisting right round in the headset, raise Settings → Controls → Head turn sensitivity.

Numbers are illustrative, inspired by harbour porpoise biosonar and hearing and tuned for playability. They are not a calibrated model.
